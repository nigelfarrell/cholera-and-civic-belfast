Cholera in Belfast in 1832 and 1848–49

(This is an updated version of an article originally published by the Centre for History of Medicine in Ireland Blog 2013: https://historyofmedicineinireland.blogspot.com/2013/09/cholera-in-belfast-1832-and-184849-by.html)

Arrival and medical understanding of cholera

On 28 February 1832, Bernard Murtagh, a 34-year-old cooper living in a lodging house on Quay Lane, Belfast—a narrow street near the River Lagan—became violently ill. Described as a man of “irregular habits,” he had suffered from diarrhoea for two or three days previously but had reported no other symptoms when he went to bed after his usual supper of stirabout and milk. Around midnight his condition deteriorated sharply as he experienced, acute vomiting and diarrhoea. The violently expelled fluid was described as whitish, resembling milk, or meal and water. By the morning, Bernard was also suffering from intense cramps. He was seen by Surgeon McBurney and members of the Belfast Board of Health, who found him in a state of extreme collapse: profoundly weak, extremely cold, and without a perceptible pulse at the wrist. A mustard emetic was administered around midday after which he appeared to revive slightly. However, this improvement was only temporary, and he died between 7 and 8 p.m. that evening, some nineteen hours after becoming ill.

High Street Belfast c.1831. A water cart can be seen to the right of the picture Source: Ulster Museum IC/High St/831

Bernard had become the first recognised victim in Ireland to have died from what was then perceived by westerners as a new and alarming global pandemic that had originated from the East: Asiatic cholera. Noted for its severity, rapidity and high mortality, cholera had long been endemic in India before spreading across Asia after 1817 and into Europe after 1829. Its signature symptoms were both distinctive and terrifying: violent vomiting and diarrhoea, resembling rice water or husks, was typically accompanied by agonising cramps, muscular spasms, a weakened pulse, low temperature, and a blue tinge to the nails and skin. Death could follow within hours.

The disease is now understood to be caused by the microorganism Vibrio cholerae, usually transmitted through ingestion of water contaminated by infected sewage. In nineteenth-century Irish towns, where sanitation was limited, this made cholera especially deadly.

Cholera Mortality in Ireland

Across Ireland, around forty per cent of cholera cases between 1832 and 1833 were fatal, with some outbreaks reaching seventy-six per cent mortality. In 1848–49 mortality was often higher due to famine weakness. Belfast in 1832 recorded 14.8 per cent mortality, lower than Dublin and Cork.

Belfast’s Urban Environment

Nineteenth-century Belfast was Ireland’s only industrialised town, outwardly prosperous but structurally deficient beneath the surface. Dense courts and alleys housed labouring populations with poor drainage, limited water supply, and inadequate sewerage.

Sewers often discharged directly into nearby waterways, and flooding returned waste into streets and homes, worsening sanitary conditions.

The 1832 Epidemic in Belfast

The epidemic response was led by Police Commissioners and a Board of Health, who implemented cleaning, fumigation, and relief measures. Temporary cholera hospitals were established at Belfast’s Fever Hospital under Dr Henry McCormac.

Treatments included bloodletting, calomel, opiates, and sulphuric acid. Hospital mortality was higher than community mortality, but public resistance to admission was less severe than in Dublin.

By 1832 Belfast recorded 2,831 cases and 418 deaths, a mortality rate of 14.8 per cent. While relatively successful, lack of sustained reform left the city vulnerable to future outbreaks.

The 1848–49 Cholera Epidemic

By the 1840s cholera had faded from attention due to famine crises. When it returned in 1848 there had been little medical advancement. Response now depended on local authorities and reformers such as Dr Andrew Malcolm.

A Sanitary Committee was established, issuing orders, improving drainage, and distributing bedding and aid. However, public reluctance to enter hospitals reduced effectiveness.

The epidemic caused 3,538 cases and 1,163 deaths, with a mortality rate of 33 per cent.

Although civic reform had progressed, major sanitation deficits remained in the poorest districts.

Later outbreaks in 1853 and 1866 showed reduced cases but persistent mortality due to limited medical understanding.
